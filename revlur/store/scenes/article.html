The quiet science of a sourdough starter

By Mara Ellison · 9 min read · Updated this week

A sourdough starter looks like very little: a jar of flour and water that bubbles faintly on the counter. Inside it, though, is a small and surprisingly stable ecosystem, with wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria living together in a way that bakers have relied on for thousands of years.

The yeast does the work you can see. It eats the sugars released from the flour and gives off carbon dioxide, which is what stretches the dough and fills it with bubbles. The bacteria work more quietly. They produce the acids that give sourdough its tang, and they keep the mixture acidic enough that most unwelcome microbes cannot take hold.

Why feeding schedules matter

Every feeding resets the clock. Fresh flour gives the microbes new food, and the water dilutes the acid that has built up since the last meal. A starter fed often stays mild and active, while one left alone slowly turns sharper as the acidity climbs and the yeast runs short of sugar.

This is why two bakers can use the same flour and still get different loaves. Temperature changes how fast everything moves, so a warm kitchen speeds the cycle up and a cool one stretches it out. Neither is wrong, but each calls for a different rhythm of feeding and a different eye for when the dough is ready.

Reading the signs

The most reliable signal is not the clock but the starter itself. When it has roughly doubled, the surface is domed and full of bubbles, and the smell is pleasantly sour rather than sharp, it is at its peak. Past that point it collapses back on itself, and the dough you make from it will rise less well.

Keep notes for the first few weeks. Write down the time you fed it, the room temperature and how long it took to peak. Within a month you will have a pattern, and with it the confidence to plan a bake around your starter instead of waiting on it.
