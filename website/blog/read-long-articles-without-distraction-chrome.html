How to read long articles in Chrome without distractions

November 8, 2026 · David

Long articles, documentation and reports are hard to read on the web. Sidebars, related links, ads and comments all compete with the text, and it's easy to lose your line.

1. Try Chrome's reading mode

Chrome has a built-in reading mode that shows a simplified version of the article in the side panel. Right-click the page and choose Open in reading mode. It strips out most of the clutter and lets you change the font and spacing.

It works well on news articles and blog posts. On pages that aren't classic articles, like documentation with code samples, tables, dashboards or forums, it can drop content you need, and you're reading a copy of the page rather than the page itself.

2. Close what you're not reading

Fewer tabs means fewer reasons to switch. Bookmark or save the rest for later, put the browser in full screen with F11, and silence notifications while you read.

3. Make the text a comfortable size

Small text makes you lean in and lose your line. Press Ctrl + + (Cmd + + on a Mac) until the lines are easy to read. On very wide screens a narrower browser window keeps lines short, which is easier on the eyes.

4. Read through a focus window

If the page itself is the distraction, blur everything except the part you're reading. With Revlur:

  1. Click the Revlur icon and drag a box around the text you're reading, a few lines or a paragraph tall.
  2. Press Scroll on the toolbar. The box stays in place on your screen and the page glides through it as you scroll, like reading through a window.
  3. Everything above and below is blurred, so your eyes stay on the current lines.
  4. Press Esc when you're done.

Unlike reading mode, this works on the real page, so code blocks, tables, images and links keep working inside the window. Turn the blur down with the slider if you'd like to see more of what's coming.

Which one to use

For a typical news article, reading mode is quick and good. For documentation, reports or any page where layout matters, a focus window keeps the original page and still takes the noise away. You can use the same trick to blur everything except one part of a page for presenting or teaching.
