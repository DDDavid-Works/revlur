How to highlight one part of your screen while screen sharing

November 8, 2026 · David

You share your screen, say “as you can see here”, and half the call is reading the sidebar. Here are four ways to make sure people look where you want them to.

Why pointing with the mouse doesn't work

On your screen the cursor is obvious. On theirs it's a few pixels wide, often lagging, and sometimes not captured at all. Meanwhile everything else on the page competes for attention: navigation, notifications, other tabs, numbers you didn't mean to show. The fix is to reduce what people can see, not to point harder.

1. Share a single window, not your whole screen

Most meeting apps let you share one window or one browser tab instead of the entire screen. Do this every time. It hides your desktop, your other apps and any notifications that pop up mid-demo. It's the easiest win, but it still shows the whole window.

2. Zoom the browser in

Press Ctrl + + (Cmd + + on a Mac) a few times so the part you're talking about fills more of the window. This helps people on small screens read numbers and text. The downside is that the page reflows, so layouts can jump around, and you lose the context of where that part sits on the page.

3. Use your operating system's magnifier

Windows Magnifier and macOS Zoom can enlarge the area around your cursor. They're built in and work in any app. They take some practice, though: the view follows your mouse, so small hand movements make the shared screen swim, which can be tiring to watch.

4. Blur everything except the part you're talking about

This is the approach Revlur takes. You drag a box around the part of the page that matters, and the rest of the page is blurred. People can still see the shape of the page, so they know where they are, but their eyes go straight to the sharp area.

  • Select: click the Revlur icon and drag a box around a chart, a table row or a paragraph.
  • Zoom: enlarge the selected area. A red pointer dot is drawn on the page itself, so it shows up even in screen shares that don't capture your real cursor.
  • Hold still: the page is locked in place while you talk, so an accidental scroll doesn't lose your spot. Clicks on the blurred area do nothing, so you can't open a link by mistake mid-demo.

Press Esc and the page is back exactly as it was.

Which one to use

Always share a single window. On top of that, browser zoom is fine for a quick look at small text, and blurring the rest works best when you're walking people through a busy page one piece at a time, like a dashboard in a meeting or a long document.
