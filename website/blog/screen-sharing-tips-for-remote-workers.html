Screen sharing tips for remote workers: share without oversharing

November 16, 2026 · David

In an office you turn your laptop toward a colleague and point. At home, the same moment becomes a screen share, and everything on your screen goes to everyone on the call.

If you work from home, you probably share your screen several times a day: a stand-up, a code review, a quick “can you look at this?” with a teammate, a weekly report for your manager. Most of those shares go fine. The ones that don't usually go wrong in one of two ways: something private shows up, or nobody can tell what you're pointing at.

These screen sharing tips for remote workers fix both. None of them take more than a minute to set up.

Why screen sharing from home is harder

Your work computer at home is rarely only a work computer. It has personal tabs, a family chat, a banking app, a calendar with a doctor's appointment. Work tools are full of things that aren't for everyone either: customer names in a CRM, salaries in a spreadsheet, other people's tickets in a project board, an inbox preview with a subject line you'd rather not explain.

There's also no way to point. In a room, people follow your finger. On a call, they follow whatever catches their eye, and on a busy page that's rarely the thing you're talking about.

Before you share: a 60-second checklist

  1. Share one window or one tab, not the whole screen. This is the biggest single improvement. Notifications, your taskbar and anything you switch to by accident stay off the call.
  2. Turn on Do Not Disturb. Windows has Focus, macOS has Focus modes, and most chat apps can pause notifications. A message preview sliding in mid-share is the most common slip.
  3. Close or move private tabs. Keep work tabs in a separate Chrome window or profile, so the tab strip you share doesn't show your personal email or shopping.
  4. Open the page you'll show before the call. Log in, set filters and scroll to the right spot first, so nobody watches you search through menus.
  5. Check what's on the page itself. A report or board may include rows, names or numbers that aren't for this audience. Decide now how you'll keep them out of view.

How to hide sensitive information when screen sharing

Sharing one window keeps other apps out. It doesn't help with what's inside that window. Here are the options, from simplest to most precise.

Filter or crop the data before the call

If the private part is a set of rows or columns, filter it out or make a copy of the view without it. This is the safest option, but it takes time and you have to remember to do it for each meeting.

Zoom the browser in

Pressing Ctrl and + (or Cmd and + on a Mac) makes the content larger, which pushes some of the page off screen. It's quick, but the layout reflows and you don't control exactly what's hidden.

Blur everything except the part you're talking about

This is what I built Revlur for. You drag a box around the part of the page you want to show, and everything else in the tab is blurred. Colleagues can see the shape of the page, so they know where they are, but they can't read the names, numbers or messages around it.

  • The selected area works normally, so you can still type, scroll a table or click inside it.
  • The blurred area ignores clicks, so you can't open a link or a record by accident while you talk.
  • The blur strength is adjustable. A light blur keeps context; a strong one makes text unreadable.
  • Press Re-select to move to the next part of the page, and Esc to turn it off.

Keep colleagues looking at the right thing

Privacy is half the problem. The other half is attention. A few habits make a big difference on remote calls:

  • Say where to look. “Top right, the red number” beats “as you can see here”.
  • Show one thing at a time. Blur the rest of the page and move the clear area as you go, instead of sharing a full page and talking over it.
  • Make small text readable. Your colleagues may be on a small laptop screen or a phone. Revlur's Zoom enlarges the selected area, and a red pointer dot follows your mouse so people can see exactly what you mean, even when the meeting app doesn't show your cursor.
  • Keep the page still. While Revlur is on, the page doesn't jump around when you brush the trackpad. Press Scroll when you want to move on.

Use the same trick to focus when you're working alone

Working from home also means more distractions on the screen: chat badges, news sidebars, recommended videos. When you need to read a long document, a contract or a spec, blurring everything except the paragraph you're on makes it much easier to stay with it. There's more on that in how to read long articles in Chrome without distractions.

Questions

How do I hide sensitive information when screen sharing from home?

Share a single window or browser tab instead of your whole screen, turn on Do Not Disturb, and blur the parts of the page you aren't talking about. Revlur does the last part in Chrome with one drag.

Should I share my whole screen or one window?

One window or one tab, almost always. Sharing the whole screen shows notifications, your taskbar, other apps and anything you switch to by mistake.

Does blurring part of the page change what I can do on it?

The selected area works normally. The blurred area ignores clicks, so you can't trigger anything under it by accident. Press Esc to remove the blur.

Can Revlur blur desktop apps like Excel or Outlook?

No. Revlur is a Chrome extension and works inside web pages. It works with the web versions of tools like Outlook, Google Sheets, Jira or Salesforce, but not with desktop apps.

The short version

Share one tab, silence notifications, and only show the part of the page you're talking about. Your colleagues see what they need, and nothing else.
